## A. Foundation councils and protocol counsel

**Decision they face:** how to write, and later amend, a foundation's deed and governance rules.

**Problem statement.** Protocol projects choose the Swiss foundation because no one owns it. The same feature means no one can take it apart. A foundation is a pool of assets bound to its founding purpose (Art. 80 ZGB), run by a council that often appoints its own successors [TEST IN PHASE 3], and supervised by an authority whose job is to keep the assets serving that purpose (Art. 84 ZGB) [VERIFY scope of supervisory mandate]. Token holders have no legal role. Foundations promise to hand the network to its community, but most deeds appear to contain no mechanism to transfer control, constrain council discretion, or dissolve the foundation once the network no longer needs it [TEST IN PHASE 3]. The result is a steward with no exit. This paper maps what protocol foundation deeds and governance rules actually contain and proposes model provisions that move control toward the network over time.

- **Claim type:** descriptive (coded dataset) plus normative, measured against the foundations' own stated goal of decentralization.
- **Deliverable:** dataset; model deed and regulation provisions; a decentralization sunset checklist.
- **Strongest objection:** purpose-lock is what makes a foundation credibly neutral. Transfer and sunset clauses invite capture by whoever controls the vote. The supervisory authority may refuse purpose amendments anyway (Arts. 86, 86a ZGB) [VERIFY].

## B. Swiss supervisory authorities and policymakers

**Decision they face:** whether and how to adapt supervisory practice for protocol foundations.

**Problem statement.** Swiss foundation supervision was built for classic public-benefit foundations. It now oversees entities that hold large token treasuries and steer networks used worldwide [VERIFY which protocol foundations sit under federal vs. cantonal supervision]. Supervisors check that assets serve the stated purpose. They have no published framework for what that means when the purpose is "support the network": how to value a treasury held largely in the network's own token, how to treat council members who hold the same token, or whether any network participant can bring a supervisory complaint [VERIFY standing]. This paper argues that targeted supervisory guidance (disclosure standards, conflict rules, and recognition of progressive decentralization as a permissible purpose path) would serve Switzerland's interest in remaining the credible home for these entities.

- **Claim type:** normative, built on a descriptive account of supervisory law and practice.
- **Deliverable:** proposed guidance text.
- **Strongest objection:** supervisors do not regulate business models. Special guidance signals endorsement and raises FINMA jurisdiction questions. Switzerland competes on predictability, not novelty. An American author has a weak channel into Swiss reform.

## C. US policymakers, and founders choosing where to domicile governance

**Decision they face:** what a US entity for network stewardship should look like.

**Problem statement.** US-developed networks send their governance offshore because US law offers no ownerless, purpose-bound entity that can steward an open network [argue; VERIFY against current US statutes]. The Swiss foundation became the default by inheritance, not design. It brings Swiss constraints with it: purpose lock, foreign supervision, and legal machinery largely invisible to the token holders who rely on it. This paper uses the Swiss foundation as a case study to specify which features a US entity should copy, which it should reject, and how recent US DAO entity statutes, such as Wyoming's DUNA (enacted 2024) [VERIFY], measure up.

- **Claim type:** comparative plus normative. Continues the 2024 research agenda ("To the Moon…or Switzerland").
- **Deliverable:** design requirements for a US stewardship entity.
- **Strongest objection:** Switzerland may no longer be the default; many newer projects use Cayman foundation companies [VERIFY trend]. US federal market-structure legislation may change the entity question [VERIFY status as of Oct. 2026]. A Swiss case study with a US ask splits the reader.
